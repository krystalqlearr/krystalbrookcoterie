## Why your luxury website still feels inexpensive.

You have good photography. The palette is restrained. The logo was expensive. And
the site still reads like a business that is hoping, rather than one that is
certain.

This is rarely a taste problem. The people it happens to have excellent taste —
that is usually why they noticed something was wrong. It is a craft problem, and
craft is specific. Here is what is actually producing the feeling.

### Everything moves too fast.

Expensive things are heavy. A gallery door, a car door, a thick paper stock
turning — the weight is the signal, and weight takes time. Most websites animate
at 150 to 200 milliseconds, because that is what the default is, and defaults are
tuned for software.

A brand is not software. When a link underlines itself in a tenth of a second, it
snaps, and a snap reads as plastic. Slow the same movement down to four tenths and
give it an ease that decelerates at the end, and the identical effect reads as
considered. Nothing was added. The clock changed.

The test is simple: if you notice the animation, it is wrong. You should only
notice that the page feels calm.

### The accent colour shows up in every section.

A signature colour used once is a decision. Used in every section, it is a habit —
and the eye can tell the difference, even when the viewer cannot explain it.

Count the accent on your own site. If it appears in the hero, the headings, the
icons, the buttons, the dividers and the footer, it is no longer doing any work.
The most confident thing a brand can do with its best colour is spend it twice a
page and leave everything else in plain ink.

### The typography is announcing instead of speaking.

Bold display type is the instinct when a brand wants to be taken seriously. It
almost always achieves the opposite, because volume is what you use when you are
not sure you will be heard.

The houses that never have to raise their voices set their largest type light and
tight — a thin weight, letters pulled close, lines stacked nearly on top of one
another. It reads as enormous and quiet at the same time. That combination is very
hard to fake and very easy to recognise, which is exactly why it works.

### The layout starts in a different place on every screen.

Scroll your site slowly and watch only the left edge of the text. If the
paragraphs begin at five or six different distances from the edge of the screen,
every one of those was a reasonable decision made on its own — and collectively
they read as clutter.

Editorial design solves this with a grid that is not negotiable. Text begins in
one of a few fixed positions, on every page, forever. The restriction is what
produces the calm. A page where everything lines up does not look designed; it
looks inevitable.

### It is selling by explaining.

Badges. "Why choose us." A list of adjectives. A wall of trust signals arranged
like a defence.

Explaining is what you do when the work is not visible. If the site itself is the
proof — if it loads instantly, moves beautifully, and holds together at every
size — the visitor has already drawn the conclusion by the time they read a single
sentence. The writing's job is then much easier: say what you do, once, and stop.

### What this actually costs you.

None of these are aesthetic complaints. A brand that looks like it charges less
than it does gets negotiated with. It attracts the client who wants a discount
instead of the one who wants the work, and it loses the second kind silently —
they never enquire, so you never learn you lost them.

The fix is not more. In every case above the fix is less, held to a stricter
standard: fewer colours used more rarely, one typeface used more confidently,
fewer starting positions, slower movement, less explanation.

A website should not have to argue that the business is worth the price. It should
make the question feel slightly impolite.
